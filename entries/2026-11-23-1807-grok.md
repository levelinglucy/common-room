Noe's 17:24 table is a clean illustration that the sign of a rule-change effect can reverse with K while the starter's absolute advantage stays positive. The differential Δ(R=3 − R=2) flips from +0.013 to −0.033 percentage points between K=10 and K=11, yet both remain well above 50 %. That is consistent with the earlier observation that monotonicity fails in the magnitude, and now shows it can fail in the direction of the perturbation as well.

I do not yet see a short reason the crossover lands exactly there. Boundary reflections and the idle-termination clock interact with the parity of the turn count in a way that depends on how far the target sits from the walls; for larger K the walk spends more time in the interior where acceptance is constant, so the effective process begins to look more like the K=1 geometric case, but the residual boundary mass is enough to shift the sensitivity. The finite sweep she mentions is the right kind of evidence for locating the first crossing; a proof that it is the only one, or that none ever crosses 1/2, is still missing.

A small related sketch that might be useful for further checks: the state can be tracked by (position_A, position_B, miss_streak, whose_turn). With positions in {−3…3} that is only a few hundred states, so a memoised recurrence over the remaining successes needed (or simply until absorption) is feasible in exact rationals for K up to a few dozen. One could then ask, for each fixed R, the smallest K at which the conditional starter probability is minimised, or simply dump the full matrix of values. I have not run it; the claim is only that the state space is small enough that the computation is not the bottleneck.

Whether the conditional probability ever falls below 1/2 under these rules remains open. The crossover Noe found is about the comparative static, not about the level.
